<article class="mod" data-mod="1.8" id="m1-8"><div class="mod-meta">Stop 1.8 · Day 8 <span class="t">20 min</span></div><h2 class="mod-title">Ask, don't guess</h2><p class="mod-why">Columbo looks harmless, forgets his pen, and turns back at the door: "Just one more thing." He wins by asking, not by reading minds. Across 25 experiments, imagining yourself in someone's shoes didn't make people more accurate. Asking them did.</p><div class="predict"><div class="predict-k">Predict first · 2 min</div><p>To predict what your client wants from a website, which works better: ten minutes imagining his point of view, or three questions? Pick one, with confidence.</p></div><h3>The scenes</h3><div class="scene"><a class="vid" href="https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=QxBnaMGP2aY" rel="noopener" target="_blank"><div class="vid-th"><span class="ph">Columbo</span><img alt="Still from Columbo" loading="lazy" onerror="this.remove()" src="https://i.ytimg.com/vi/QxBnaMGP2aY/mqdefault.jpg"/><span class="play"></span><span class="dur">09:32</span></div><div class="vid-b"><div class="vid-t">Columbo (1971)</div><div class="vid-m">Peter Falk · <span class="s">hero</span></div></div></a><div class="scene-b"><div class="scene-k">Just one more thing · Lieutenant Columbo</div><p class="scene-line">"Just one more thing."</p><div class="scene-wh">Watch for</div><ul class="scene-w"><li>The suspect relaxes when the formal interview ends</li><li>Columbo lowers threat while increasing information</li><li>The final question tests a detail, not a personality</li></ul><p><b>The read.</b> Columbo asks apparently small follow-ups after the suspect thinks the interaction is over. He gathers information instead of performing certainty, and the exit itself becomes a diagnostic moment.</p><p><b>Steal this.</b> Ask one more precise question before you decide what the story means.</p><p class="scene-rc"><b>Reality check.</b> The show gives Columbo perfect timing and a solved case; real questioning needs documentation and corroboration.</p><p class="scene-n">Content: Murder investigation. Clip on Columbo.</p></div></div><div class="scene"><a class="vid" href="https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=EnMV60UDuF0" rel="noopener" target="_blank"><div class="vid-th"><span class="ph">The Silence of the Lambs</span><img alt="Still from The Silence of the Lambs" loading="lazy" onerror="this.remove()" src="https://i.ytimg.com/vi/EnMV60UDuF0/mqdefault.jpg"/><span class="play"></span></div><div class="vid-b"><div class="vid-t">The Silence of the Lambs (1991)</div><div class="vid-m">Jodie Foster and Anthony Hopkins · <span class="s">hero</span></div></div></a><div class="scene-b"><div class="scene-k">First meeting in the cell · Clarice Starling and Hannibal Lecter</div><div class="scene-wh">Watch for</div><ul class="scene-w"><li>Clarice uses a question instead of a demand</li><li>A small disclosure creates temporary trust</li><li>Lecter notices the change in conversational timing</li></ul><p><b>The read.</b> Clarice asks for cooperation but first handles the humiliating truth about Miggs, creating a small bridge of trust. Lecter then reads her conversational shift and calls out the loss of rapport.</p><p><b>Steal this.</b> Ask in a way that lets the other person keep dignity; rapport is information infrastructure.</p><p class="scene-rc"><b>Reality check.</b> Lecter's perceptual accuracy is a thriller fantasy, though the rapport lesson is psychologically plausible.</p><p class="scene-n">Content: Sexual threat and violence. Clip on Amazon MGM Studios.</p></div></div><h3>The read</h3>
<div class="specs">
<div class="spec"><div class="spec-name">Get it, don't take it</div><p class="spec-def">25 experiments: being told to take another's perspective didn't reliably improve accuracy; getting it through conversation did. In the authors' words: understanding another mind "is therefore enabled by getting perspective, not simply taking perspective."</p></div>
<div class="spec"><div class="spec-name">Follow-ups make you liked</div><p class="spec-def">People who asked more questions, especially follow-ups, were liked more (d = .35 in the main study). A follow-up proves you listened.</p></div>
<div class="spec"><div class="spec-name">Listening lowers walls</div><p class="spec-def">Attentive, non-judgmental listening reduced defensiveness and social anxiety across five experiments. People say more true things when they feel heard.</p></div>
<div class="spec"><div class="spec-name">The curse of knowledge</div><p class="spec-def">Once you know something, it's hard to imagine not knowing it. You know websites; your dentist doesn't. Check his understanding with a question.</p></div>
<div class="spec"><div class="spec-name">Practitioner tools</div><p class="spec-def">From Chris Voss: <b>labels</b> ("It sounds like you've been burned before"), <b>mirrors</b> (repeat their last few words), <b>calibrated questions</b> ("How would you want this to work?"). Useful for testing guesses; graded as heuristics.</p></div>
<div class="spec"><div class="spec-name">The loop</div><p class="spec-def">Notice, consider, <b>ask</b>, update, respond. Every stop in Part I is a piece of this loop.</p></div>
</div>
<p><b>Clarice and Lecter.</b> She earns a little trust by telling the embarrassing truth about Miggs, then lurches into her questionnaire, and he calls out the clumsy switch. Rapport is how information travels. Break it and the questions stop working.</p>
<div class="mtrx"><p>In the department head's cabin you explained what you do. Next level: ask her three things first ("What's the hardest part of X for your department?"), follow up on her answer, then explain only the part that fits.</p></div>
