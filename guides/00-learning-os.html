Why Field Guide 01 had gaps, and what changed

You asked for no gaps and you were right to be annoyed. Here is what went wrong, in plain words, because the fix only makes sense once the cause is clear.

Version 1 of the tech guide was built top-down. I asked: "what will make Kevin sound sharp in a room?" and picked what impresses: Rust, agents, LLMs, stacks. That is a salience filter, and salience filters skip the boring, load-bearing stuff. Cookies, analytics, a CMS, email that lands. Exactly what a dental client asks about on day one.

You said you would have missed so much because of "recklessness and laziness". No. You cannot ask about a word you have never heard. That is not a character flaw, it is a method problem. And effort does not fix it either: I was at max effort and still missed them, because listing from memory is the broken step. Even experts leave out most of what they know when they explain it freely. In one careful study, surgeons describing a procedure they do routinely left out about 71% of the knowledge steps, 51% of the actions and 73% of the decisions (Sullivan et al., 2014, a small single-procedure study, so treat the numbers as a warning, not a law).

So the fix had to be a procedure, not more effort. For version 3 I built a 380-concept inventory from 14 independent sources (syllabi, standards, certifications, official docs), tiered it, then checked every concept against the guide's body text, not the glossary.

Why reading everything still feels empty

You described it exactly: lots of input, a hollow feeling after. There are four separate causes, and each has its own fix. Test which ones are yours.

1 · Fluency

Smooth prose feels like understanding. Psychologists call it the illusion of explanatory depth: people rate their grasp of how a zip or a toilet works highly, then try to explain it step by step and their rating collapses (Rozenblit and Keil, 2002). Reading builds recognition, not reconstruction.

2 · Inert knowledge

You can quote a concept, but it never fires when a client case shows up in different clothes. The philosopher Whitehead called these "inert ideas": received, never used, tested or combined. Transfer to a new context is not automatic.

3 · No map

Experts do not just know more facts. Chi, Feltovich and Glaser (1981) showed physics experts sort problems by the principle underneath ("conservation of energy"), novices by surface ("it has a ramp"). Without a map, more reading means more disconnected islands.

4 · No loop

No test, no error log, no delayed check, no repair. You cannot see what changed, so it feels like nothing did. Plus a growing pile of unread tabs creates chronic "not done".

Physics flashback. You know this one. Students pass mechanics exams and still believe a thrown ball carries a "force of the throw". Hestenes built the Force Concept Inventory in 1992 to catch exactly that, with wrong answers built from common-sense misconceptions. Hake's survey of 6,542 students found conceptual gains of about 0.23 in lecture courses vs 0.48 in interactive ones (observational, not randomised). Passing is not understanding. That is why your guides test the misconception, not the definition.

"I don't trust the feeling of getting it. I trust what I can explain tomorrow with the page closed."

"Input is necessary. Output is where it sticks."

Stop 1.3 4 min

Myths to drop

The popular stuff that sounds scientific. Drop these and you free up time for what works.

"I'm a visual learner"

Preferences exist, but matching teaching to a "style" has no adequate evidence (Pashler et al., 2008). Everyone learns better with words plus good diagrams.

"10,000 hours makes an expert"

Ericsson never claimed a threshold. Practice explains very different amounts of skill across fields, under 1% in professions.

"Rereading and highlighting = studying"

Rated low utility (Dunlosky et al., 2013). Yes, I built you a highlighter. Use it to mark what to test yourself on, not as the learning.

"Speed reading with full comprehension"

There is a speed and accuracy trade-off (Rayner et al., 2016). Skim to orient, slow down to understand.

"We remember 10% of what we read, 90% of what we do"

The learning pyramid percentages have no credible original study behind them.

"The Feynman technique is proven"

Explaining simply is retrieval plus self-explanation plus gap-finding, all supported. The branded four-step package was never trialled as such. Use it, label it a heuristic.

"The algorithm guarantees retention"

Anki's FSRS predicts review timing better than old SM-2 in benchmarks, but no randomised trial shows users learn more. A great scheduler cannot fix a bad card.

"A high quiz score proves I covered the field"

A quiz samples. It can miss whole areas and test recognition only. Coverage needs a map; mastery needs changed-surface questions.

"An expert can just list what I need"

Experts omit most automated knowledge when listing freely. Including me. That is why I triangulate syllabi now instead of listing from memory.

"Make it hard, difficulty helps"

Only desirable difficulty: effortful retrieval and discrimination. Missing prerequisites or overload is just difficulty.

"Learning styles are a preference, not a mechanism. I use words and diagrams, and I test myself."

Why you cannot just ask "what am I missing?"

This is the core problem behind your frustration, and the reason the answer is a procedure.

Known unknowns are cheap: you hear "serverless", you ask. Unknown knowns surface when you try to teach. But unknown unknowns cannot be found by asking, by trying harder, or by asking an expert to list everything, because they all start from someone's memory. The only thing that works is triangulation: lay several independent maps of the field on top of each other and look at what any of them has that you do not.

"Nobody can list what they don't know. So I don't list, I diff against syllabi."

The five gap passes

Boundary pass

What is just outside the scope, and where does the model break?

Counterexample pass

Cases that break the rule, rival schools, failed replications.

Error pass

Real novice mistakes, support questions, exam distractors, incident reports.

Practice pass

Compare with tasks professionals actually do (job taxonomies like O*NET and ESCO).

Transfer pass

Same idea in a different context, person, time delay or goal. Does it still fire?

    Stop 3.3 3 min

    Know that you know

    Calibration: matching your confidence to reality. It is the skill that makes every other skill trustworthy.

    Confidence right after reading tracks fluency. Confidence after a delay tracks retrieval. In the meta-analysis by Rhodes and Tauber (2011), delayed judgments were dramatically better at predicting which items you would remember (gamma about .93), even though the delay itself barely improved memory. So the delay is not for learning. It is for knowing what you learned.

    Overconfident

    Predicted 5, scored 2. The danger zone: you would have bluffed in a meeting. Top of tomorrow's review.

    Underconfident

    Predicted 2, scored 5. Fine for learning, bad for selling. Practise saying it out loud.

    Calibrated

    Prediction matches score. Now you can trust your gut on this topic.

    "I know which of my opinions are earned and which are borrowed."

      Stop 4.1 3 min

      Your two quotes, unlearned

      Two lines shaped how you think about learning: "learn, unlearn, relearn" and Darwin on adaptability. Both are slightly wrong. The real versions are better, and fixing them is your first unlearn.

      The "Khosla" line

      It is not from Khosla. Alvin Toffler wrote in Future Shock (1970): "By instructing students how to learn, unlearn and relearn, a powerful new dimension can be added to education." Right next to it he quotes the psychologist Herbert Gerjuoy: "Tomorrow's illiterate will not be the man who can't read; he will be the man who has not learned how to learn." The famous one-liner is a later mash-up of the two.

      The "Darwin" line

      "It is not the strongest of the species that survives, but the most adaptable" is not in Darwin. It traces to a 1963 speech by the business professor Leon Megginson, paraphrasing Darwin.

      What Darwin actually did

      Better than the quote. In his autobiography: "whenever a published fact, a new observation or thought came across me, which was opposed to my general results, to make a memorandum of it without fail and at once; for I had found by experience that such facts and thoughts were far more apt to escape from the memory than the favourable ones." That is the unlearning habit, written down in the 1800s.

      Evolution, applied to a mind

      Natural selection works on populations over generations, not on one animal choosing to adapt. The honest version for one person came from the psychologist Donald Campbell (1960): blind variation and selective retention. Try many variants, test them against reality, keep what survives. That is exactly the learning loop: generate, test, retain.

      Notice what just happened. You held two beliefs with confidence, met better evidence, and replaced them with stronger versions while keeping what was true (adaptability matters; learning to learn matters). That is unlearning. It did not feel like losing anything.

      "Toffler said learn, unlearn, relearn. Darwin's real trick was writing down what contradicted him."

      Stop 4.2 5 min

      Curiosity, the ignition

      You asked me to make you curious. Research says curiosity is not a personality you have or lack; it is a state you can trigger, with a known recipe and one big trap.

      It is a gap you can see

      George Loewenstein (1994): curiosity is the feeling of noticing a gap between what you know and what you want to know. No visible gap, no curiosity. A gap that looks unclosable gives up instead.

      Two flavours

      Interest curiosity is the pleasure of exploring. Deprivation curiosity is the itch of not knowing (Litman). Both work. The itch is stronger for a specific answer.

      It makes things stick

      In Kang et al. (2009), trivia answers people were curious about were remembered better one to two weeks later. In Gruber, Gelman and Ranganath (2014), people even remembered unrelated faces better if they saw them while curious. Curiosity opens a memory window.

      Predicting sparks it

      Committing to a guess before the answer raised curiosity and memory compared with just thinking about the topic (Brod and Breitwieser, 2019). This is why every stop asks you to predict first.

      The trap: seductive details

      Fun but irrelevant facts raise interest and lower learning, because they steal attention from the mechanism (Harp and Mayer, 1998; Rey, 2012). A hook must carry the idea, not decorate it.

      Not clickbait

      "You won't believe..." creates a gap with nothing worth learning behind it. Honest question, honest answer, promptly.

      What changes in every guide from now

      1. Each stop opens with a real puzzle or decision ("why does X happen despite Y?"), not a "did you know".
      2. You predict and rate your confidence before the reveal.
      3. The answer comes quickly, mechanism first, decoration later or never.
      4. Each stop ends with one new question the answer opens up, for your question ledger.

        Stop 4.3 5 min

        How to unlearn

        Unlearning is harder than learning, because the old idea does not leave when you are told it is wrong. Here is why, and a protocol that works.

        Corrections leak

        Telling someone a claim is false reduces its influence but does not erase it: the continued influence effect (Lewandowsky et al., 2012; Ecker et al., 2022). The old model keeps getting used unless something better takes its place.

        Refutation works

        Lessons that state the misconception, explain why it fails and give the better model beat plain explanation: about g = 0.41 across 44 comparisons (Schroeder and Kucera, 2022).

        Einstellung

        Luchins (1942): people who learned one method for water-jar puzzles kept using it even when a simpler one existed. Chess experts who saw a familiar solution missed a better one (Bilalic, McLeod and Gobet, 2008). Knowing a way can block a better way.

        Expert entrenchment

        Deep expertise can harden into rigidity when the world changes (Dane, 2010). It is a risk, not a rule: experts were actually less trapped than weaker players.

        Think in probabilities

        Your ML brain already knows this: beliefs are priors, evidence updates them. Forecasting tournaments found training in probabilistic thinking and updating improved accuracy by roughly 8 to 10% (Mellers et al., 2014; Atanasov et al., 2020).

        Intellectual humility

        Not self-doubt. Knowing where your knowledge ends, saying how sure you are, and naming what would change your mind (Leary et al., 2017).

        Any age, honestly

        You want to be able to learn anything at any age. The evidence says yes, with honest limits, and it says your existing knowledge is your biggest asset.

        Adult brains change

        Learning to juggle changed grey matter in motion areas within months (Draganski et al., 2004), and adults around 60 could learn it too, with similar changes (Boyke et al., 2008). London taxi drivers have larger posterior hippocampi (Maguire et al., 2000; a correlation, not proof of cause).

        Hard, new and sustained wins

        In the Synapse Project, older adults who spent about 15 hours a week for three months learning demanding new skills (digital photography, quilting) improved episodic memory more than those doing easy social activities (Park et al., 2014).

        Languages

        Grammar learning ability stays high until about 17, then slowly declines; native-like grammar usually needs an early start (Hartshorne, Tenenbaum and Pinker, 2018, 669,498 people). Adults still reach strong fluency, just through different routes.

        Your unfair advantage

        Prior knowledge is what new knowledge hangs on. Your physics, maths and ML give you ready-made schemas (rates, systems, feedback, probability) for business, psychology and tech. Use them on purpose: ask "what is this the physics of?"

        What does not work

        Brain-training apps improve the trained game, not general intelligence. "Rewire your whole brain" is marketing. Change is specific to what you practise.

        "Speed peaks early. Knowledge compounds. I'm building the part that compounds."

        Mental strength that actually works

        Discipline, grit, mindset: the self-help shelf is half solid, half myth. Here is the verdict on each, and the version that works for a 20-minute daily habit.

        ClaimVerdictEvidence
        Discipline is willpower, a fuel tank that runs outDoesn't hold upA 23-lab replication found almost no ego-depletion effect (d = 0.04, Hagger et al., 2016)
        It takes 21 days to form a habitDoesn't hold upMedian about 66 days, range 18 to 254; missing a day didn't reset it (Lally et al., 2010)
        If-then plans turn goals into actionHolds up"If it's 7:30 after tea, I open the guide": d about 0.65 across 94 tests (Gollwitzer and Sheeran, 2006)
        Picture the obstacle, not just the dream (WOOP)Holds up, modestlyWish, outcome, obstacle, plan: g about 0.34 (Wang et al., 2021)
        Grit predicts successPartlyPerseverance helps, but grit mostly overlaps with conscientiousness (Credé et al., 2017)
        A growth mindset changes achievementPartlySmall effects overall, near zero in the best studies, bigger for struggling students (Macnamara and Burgoyne, 2023; Yeager et al., 2019)
        Self-control in childhood predicts life outcomesHolds up, as a correlationDunedin study of 1,037 people to age 32 (Moffitt et al., 2011); the marshmallow effect shrinks a lot after controls (Watts et al., 2018)
        Sleep locks in learningHolds upSleep consolidates memory (Rasch and Born, 2013). Learning late and sleeping short wastes the session
        Exercise makes you smarterPartlySmall real benefit, about SMD 0.22 in adults over 50 (Northey et al., 2018); not a genius switch
        Affirmations alone create successDoesn't hold upPositive fantasy without obstacles and plans doesn't deliver; WOOP works because it adds the obstacle
        Everything is achievableDoesn't hold up as statedSurvivorship bias hides those who tried and failed. The true version: far more is learnable than you think, and you control inputs, not outcomes
        Design the environment

        Strong people mostly have easier environments: fixed time, fixed place, phone in another room. Make the guide the easiest thing to open, the feed the hardest.

        Plan the lapse

        Habits survive missed days (Lally). The rule: never miss twice. A 5-minute session counts as a win.

        Motivation that lasts

        Self-determination theory: autonomy (you chose the topic), competence (you can see progress), relatedness (you teach it to someone). Every guide is now built to feed all three.

        Franklin's chart

        Benjamin Franklin tracked 13 virtues in a little book: one virtue per week, a black spot for each slip, four cycles a year. He admitted it was burdensome and still credited it. Your weak-spot log is the same idea, 300 years later.
