How to run it: twelve weeks

One life a week, and a small daily stack. The aim is not to finish the guide. The aim is that in twelve weeks some of these are things you just do.

Monday: read that week's life (3 minutes) and start its drill. Every day: run your stack of three drills from Part II, about 12 minutes. Sunday: the evidence ledger review and a pre-mortem on the week ahead. If you miss a day, the only rule is never miss twice: habits survive single misses (Lally et al., 2010).

Seneca: the nightly trial

One of the richest men in Rome, adviser to an emperor, put himself on trial every single night. Why would a powerful man do that to himself?

c. 4 BCE to 65 CE · Roman Stoic philosopher, playwright and adviser to the emperor Nero · Temperance

“I examine my whole day, and go back over all that I have said and done; I hide nothing from myself, I pass nothing by.”

Seneca, On Anger (De Ira) III.36. Translations vary slightly. Read the source
The practice

A nightly review. When the lamp was out, Seneca went over the day: what he said, what he did, where he lost his temper, and what he would do differently. Then he forgave himself and slept.

Why they did it

He wrote it as a routine he actually kept, inside a book about controlling anger. The point was not guilt. It was rehearsal: catch the error while it is fresh, decide the better response, and meet it prepared tomorrow.

What the research says Strong

The modern twin is recorded self-monitoring plus if-then planning. Monitoring progress raised goal attainment (d = 0.40 across 138 trials, Harkin et al., 2016), and if-then plans are among the best-supported behaviour tools there are (Gollwitzer and Sheeran, 2006).

Drop this myth

“Luck is what happens when preparation meets opportunity” is all over the internet as Seneca. There is no Seneca source for it; the wording is far later.

Run it · 3 min

Before bed, answer three questions in your notes: What did I do well? Where did I lose control? What will I do next time? Turn the last answer into one line: “If [situation], then I will [response].”

“Every night I review the day like a case file: what worked, what slipped, and my if-then for tomorrow.”

Marcus Aurelius: notes to himself

The most powerful man on earth kept a notebook he never meant anyone to read. What was an emperor writing to himself?

121 to 180 CE · Roman emperor who wrote a private notebook in Greek, now called the Meditations · Temperance

“If thou art pained by any external thing, it is not this thing that disturbs thee, but thy own judgement about it.”

Marcus Aurelius, Meditations 8.47, George Long translation. Read the source
The practice

Short private notes of self-correction. He describes an event plainly, separates it from the judgment he added, and reminds himself what his principles require. His mornings start the same way (Book 2.1): “Begin the morning by saying to thyself, I shall meet with the busy-body, the ungrateful, arrogant...” He rehearsed the hard parts of the day before they arrived.

Why they did it

Much of it was written on military campaigns, under enormous pressure. It is not a manual for others; it is a man arguing with himself, again and again, about the same few weaknesses.

What the research says Moderate

The modern twins are cognitive reappraisal (changing the interpretation of an event, d = 0.36 in 306 comparisons, Webb, Miles and Sheeran, 2012) and self-distancing (Kross and Ayduk, 2017). The exact Stoic exercise has not been tested in trials, so treat it as a moderate, well-grounded prompt.

Drop this myth

“You have power over your mind, not outside events. Realize this, and you will find strength.” It is on every poster, and it is not in the Meditations, in any classic translation. It is a modern paraphrase of the real 8.47 line above.

Run it · 3 min

Write one irritating event in neutral words, as a camera would record it. Underline the judgment you added (“he's disrespecting me”). Write one action you control. Tomorrow morning, before your calls, name the two hardest people you expect to meet and how you will respond.

“The event is the event. The story I add is my part, and that's the part I can change.”

Benjamin Franklin: the virtue chart

At about 20, Franklin set out to become morally perfect, and failed. So why did he say, decades later, that the attempt made him a better and happier man?

1706 to 1790 · American printer, inventor, entrepreneur and statesman · Temperance

“I made a little book, in which I allotted a page for each of the virtues... I might mark, by a little black spot, every fault I found upon examination to have been committed respecting that virtue upon that day.”

Benjamin Franklin, Autobiography, Part Two. Read the source
The practice

A weekly virtue chart. Thirteen virtues (temperance, silence, order, resolution, frugality, industry, sincerity, justice, moderation, cleanliness, tranquillity, chastity, humility), one page each, seven columns for the days. Each week he gave one virtue his full attention while still marking slips on all of them, cycling through the thirteen four times a year. His daily schedule opened with “What good shall I do this day?” and closed with “What good have I done to-day?”

Why they did it

He admitted he never reached perfection and that the system was burdensome. But he wrote that he was, “by the endeavour, a better and a happier man than I otherwise should have been.” A printer's approach to character: measure, focus, repeat.

What the research says Strong

This is recorded progress monitoring, the strongest finding in this guide: d = 0.40 for goal attainment across 138 randomised trials, with bigger effects when progress is physically recorded or made public (Harkin et al., 2016).

Drop this myth

“Beer is proof that God loves us and wants us to be happy” is not Franklin's wording. It is a modern rewrite of a 1779 letter of his about wine, rain and vineyards.

Run it · 2 min

Pick one trait for this week (for you, maybe “follow-up within 24 hours” or “no phone before the daily 20”). Each evening mark yesterday 0, 1 or 2. Each morning answer Franklin's question in one observable act: “What good shall I do this day?”

“I run Franklin's chart: one trait a week, marked every night. It's boring, and it works.”

Charles Darwin: the golden rule

Why did the man who changed biology keep a special list of evidence against his own theory?

1809 to 1882 · English naturalist who worked out evolution by natural selection · Wisdom

“I had, also, during many years followed a golden rule, namely, that whenever a published fact, a new observation or thought came across me, which was opposed to my general results, to make a memorandum of it without fail and at once; for I had found by experience that such facts and thoughts were far more apt to escape from the memory than favourable ones.”

Charles Darwin, Autobiography, in The Life and Letters of Charles Darwin (1887), vol. 1. Read the source
The practice

A contrary-evidence log. Anything that cut against his theory got written down immediately, because he had noticed his memory quietly dropped inconvenient facts and kept the flattering ones.

Why they did it

He spent more than twenty years gathering evidence before publishing On the Origin of Species (1859). Writing down the objections first meant his book answered the strongest arguments against it before critics could raise them.

What the research says Moderate

This is a deliberate fix for confirmation bias. “Consider the opposite” prompts reduced biased reading of evidence in experiments (Lord, Lepper and Preston, 1984), and recorded self-monitoring helps generally (Harkin et al., 2016). Darwin's exact rule has never been trialled, so: moderate.

Drop this myth

Two in one. “It is not the strongest of the species that survives, but the most adaptable” is not Darwin; it traces to a 1963 speech by Leon Megginson. And “survival of the fittest” was coined by Herbert Spencer in 1864; Darwin adopted it in a later edition.

Run it · 3 min

Write today's working belief about your business in one line (for example “dental clinics choose on price”). Add the newest fact that cuts against it. Say what you'd check to settle it. Keep these in one running list, and reread it monthly.

“I keep a list of evidence against my own plan. If it stays empty, I'm not looking hard enough.”

Stop 1.5 3 min

Richard Feynman: don't fool yourself

A Nobel physicist's advice to graduating scientists was not “be brilliant”. It was a warning about the easiest person to fool. Who did he mean?

1918 to 1988 · American physicist, Nobel laureate and famous teacher · Courage

“The first principle is that you must not fool yourself, and you are the easiest person to fool.”

Richard Feynman, “Cargo Cult Science”, Caltech commencement address, 1974. Read the source
The practice

Leaning over backwards. For any explanation he liked, he listed everything that could make it wrong and reported those details too, not just the ones that made the story work.

Why they did it

The talk mocks “cargo cult” science: rituals that look like science, with the honesty missing. His standard was integrity a level beyond ordinary honesty: report the evidence that might sink you.

What the research says Moderate

This is disconfirmation, the same family as Darwin's rule and the pre-mortem (imagining failure raised the number of reasons people found by about 30%, Klein, 2007). Moderate: well-grounded, not tested as a personal habit.

Drop this myth

“If you think you understand quantum mechanics, you don't understand quantum mechanics” is very often credited to Feynman. No reliable Feynman source has that exact sentence.

Run it · 3 min

Write the assumption your current pitch or plan depends on. Add two observations that would prove it wrong. Run the cheapest test this week (one question to five prospects) and record the result before you polish the pitch.

“Before I sell my idea to a client, I try to break it myself.”

Stop 1.6 3 min

Marie Curie: the lab record

Her laboratory notebooks from those years are still radioactive and are handled with precautions. What was in them that mattered so much?

1867 to 1934 · Polish-born physicist and chemist, the first person to win Nobel prizes in two sciences · Courage

“All the elements emitting such radiation I have termed radioactive, and the new property of matter revealed in this emission has thus received the name radioactivity.”

Marie Curie, Nobel Lecture, 11 December 1911. Read the source
The practice

A dated, exact laboratory record. She measured the radiation of substance after substance with an electrometer and wrote down every reading, including failures, rather than trusting memory. Patterns in those records pointed to new elements: polonium and radium, both announced in 1898.

Why they did it

She was working in a converted shed with little money, processing tonnes of ore by hand. The notebooks were how a slow, grinding process became a discovery: nothing depended on remembering what happened three months earlier.

What the research says Moderate

An external, written record is progress monitoring again (Harkin et al., 2016) and protects against hindsight bias. Her specific method is historical, not experimental: moderate.

Drop this myth

“Nothing in life is to be feared, it is only to be understood” is widely credited to her, but the original source is unclear. Don't put it on a slide as her exact words.

Run it · 3 min

Log one work attempt today in five fields: date, what you tried, the result, anything odd, the next test. Ban the phrase “worked on X”. For your callers' campaign: calls made, connects, booked, one odd objection, tomorrow's tweak.

“Not ‘worked on outreach’: 40 calls, 9 connects, 2 booked, one new objection. That's a lab note.”

Leonardo da Vinci: the question list

Why would the man who painted the Mona Lisa write himself a note to “describe the tongue of the woodpecker”?

1452 to 1519 · Italian painter, engineer and anatomist whose notebooks run to thousands of pages · Wisdom

“Describe the tongue of the woodpecker.”

Leonardo da Vinci, a to-do list in his anatomical notebooks, now in the Royal Collection at Windsor. Read the source
The practice

A question ledger. His notebooks are full of lists of specific questions, things to measure, books to find and experts to ask (about water, machines, anatomy), alongside drawings that answer them. Curiosity turned into errands.

Why they did it

The woodpecker's tongue really is strange: it wraps around inside the skull. Leonardo did not wait for curiosity to strike. He wrote the question down so it would get answered.

What the research says Moderate

Curiosity improves memory for the answer, and committing to questions before learning helps (Kang et al., 2009; Gruber et al., 2014; see No. 00, Stop 4.2). Leonardo's own system has never been tested, so treat it as moderate by analogy.

Drop this myth

“Simplicity is the ultimate sophistication” is printed on a thousand product launches as Leonardo. No reliable Leonardo manuscript or early source contains it.

Run it · 3 min

Write three precise questions about your current project (not “how do I grow?” but “why do clinics that open my email not reply?”). Next to each, write the person or source who could answer it and the 10-minute action that starts it. Use the question ledger in No. 00.

“I don't keep a to-do list. I keep a to-find-out list.”

Stop 1.8 3 min

Maya Angelou: the bare room

One of America's great writers rented a hotel room, had everything taken off the walls, and was at work there by 6:30 a.m. Why leave a perfectly good home to write?

1928 to 2014 · American poet, memoirist, performer and civil rights activist · Transcendence

“I rent a hotel room for a few months, leave my home at six, and try to be at work by 6:30... I insist that all things are taken off the walls. I don't want anything in there.”

Maya Angelou, interview with George Plimpton, The Paris Review, “The Art of Fiction No. 119”, 1990. Read the source
The practice

A protected place and a fixed block. A bare room with nothing on the walls, the same early start, a warm-up of reading great writing aloud (the Psalms, Paul Laurence Dunbar, James Weldon Johnson), then drafting until about one in the afternoon. In the evening she reread the morning's pages: from nine, she said, she might save two and a half or three.

Why they did it

The room did one job only; she never slept there. She also described an evening self-check that echoes Seneca: if you are honest and have a little courage, you look at yourself and say, “Hmm. I only blew it eighty-six times. Not bad.”

What the research says Strong

A stable time and place is how habits form (Lally et al., 2010), and if-then plans tied to a cue are strongly supported (d = 0.65, Gollwitzer and Sheeran, 2006; a 2024 synthesis of 642 tests confirms positive effects). The creative-output part is less studied.

Drop this myth

“There is no greater agony than bearing an untold story inside you” is credited to Angelou everywhere. It is Zora Neale Hurston's, in her 1942 memoir Dust Tracks on a Road: “There is no agony like bearing an untold story inside you.”

Run it · 5 min

As a nomad, your room changes, so make one surface the room. Clear it, phone in a bag, read four lines of something excellent, then write or build for a fixed block before email. Same cue every day: “If I've had my first tea, then I open the work, not the inbox.”

“I don't wait to feel like it. I have a time and a place, and the work starts there.”

Srinivasa Ramanujan: the letter

A clerk in Madras earning £20 a year mailed pages of equations to a Cambridge professor he had never met. What did he do that most self-taught people never do?

1887 to 1920 · Indian mathematician, largely self-taught, whose notebooks are still being mined today · Wisdom

“I have not trodden through the conventional regular course which is followed in a University course, but I am striking out a new path for myself.”

Ramanujan, letter to G. H. Hardy, 16 January 1913. Cambridge University Library. Read the source
The practice

Produce, record, then seek a qualified critic. He worked problems alone, filled notebooks with results, and then sent his best work, plainly, to someone able to judge it, saying exactly what training he lacked and asking for advice.

Why they did it

Hardy recognised the genius in the letter and brought him to Cambridge in 1914. The notebooks alone would have stayed in Madras. The letter is what connected the work to the world.

What the research says Moderate

Feedback is one of the most powerful influences on learning when it is specific and acted on (Hattie and Timperley, 2007). Self-teaching without outside critique is where blind spots grow. Moderate: strong principle, historical case.

Drop this myth

“An equation for me has no meaning unless it expresses a thought of God” is widely quoted as his. It comes second-hand, recalled by others years later. His letters are the safe source.

Run it · 5 min

Pick one piece of work you're proud of (your AI receptionist demo, a site, a pitch). Write a five-line note to someone more expert: what it is, what you're unsure about, one specific question. Send it this week.

“Self-taught is fine. Self-checked isn't. Every month, one expert sees my work.”

Florence Nightingale: the chart that saved lives

She is remembered as the Lady with the Lamp. Why did she think her most powerful tool was a chart?

1820 to 1910 · English nurse, statistician and hospital reformer · Justice

“Diagram of the Causes of Mortality in the Army in the East.”

Title of Nightingale's 1858 polar-area (“coxcomb”) diagram, British Library. Read the source
The practice

Turn observations into a picture that forces a decision. Her diagram showed that in the Crimean War far more soldiers died of preventable disease than of wounds, in a form officials could not ignore. Data first, then the argument.

Why they did it

She collected and classified mortality records, worked with statisticians, and used the charts to push army and hospital sanitation reform. In 1858 she became the first woman elected to the Royal Statistical Society.

What the research says Moderate

Graphs help when they match the decision being made (Shah, Freedman and Vekiri, 2005). The historical practice is moderate by analogy; the principle of measuring before arguing is standard in every evidence-based field.

Drop this myth

“I attribute my success to this: I never gave or took any excuse” is a popular Nightingale quote with no secure primary source. Use her charts, not the poster.

Run it · 5 min

Choose one number that matters this month (bookings per 100 calls, reply rate on DMs). Draw a tiny before and after chart by hand. Under it write one sentence: “If this holds for two more weeks, I will change X.”

“I don't argue opinions with clients. I show them one chart and one decision.”

Mahatma Gandhi: life as an experiment

Why would a man leading a nation's freedom struggle call his autobiography a series of “experiments”?

1869 to 1948 · Leader of India's independence movement through mass non-violent action · Humanity

“I have given the chapters I propose to write the title of The Story of My Experiments with Truth. These will of course include experiments with non-violence, celibacy and other principles of conduct.”

M. K. Gandhi, An Autobiography or The Story of My Experiments with Truth, Introduction, translated by Mahadev Desai. Read the source
The practice

Treat your conduct as an experiment. State a principle, test it in ordinary life (diet, money, work, conflict), record what happened, and revise. The spinning wheel he worked at daily became the symbol of that discipline.

Why they did it

He tried and dropped many practices over decades, openly. Framing them as experiments made changing course a result, not a failure, which is unlearning built into a life.

What the research says Moderate

Behavioural experiments are a core tool of cognitive behavioural therapy, and if-then planning is strongly supported (Gollwitzer and Sheeran, 2006). The life-as-experiment frame itself is untested: moderate.

Drop this myth

Two. “Be the change you wish to see in the world” is not a verbatim Gandhi line; it condenses ideas he wrote in different words. “First they ignore you, then they laugh at you...” traces to a 1918 speech by the American union organiser Nicholas Klein.

Run it · 3 min

Name one principle you want to live by (“I under-promise and over-deliver to clients”). Design this week's test of it, with something you can observe. On Sunday write the result and one adjustment. Experiment, not resolution.

“I don't make resolutions. I run experiments and keep the results.”

Stop 1.12 3 min

Charlie Munger: the latticework

One of the most successful investors of the century told business students that knowing lots of facts is almost useless. What did he think mattered instead?

1924 to 2023 · American investor, Warren Buffett's business partner at Berkshire Hathaway · Wisdom

“You can't really know anything if you just remember isolated facts and try and bang 'em back. If the facts don't hang together on a latticework of theory, you don't have them in a usable form.”

Charlie Munger, “A Lesson on Elementary, Worldly Wisdom”, USC Marshall School of Business, 14 April 1994. Read the source
The practice

A latticework of big models from many fields (incentives, compounding, base rates, supply and demand, psychology of misjudgment), applied together to every decision. And inversion: ask what would guarantee failure, then avoid it.

Why they did it

He warned that “to the man with only a hammer, every problem looks like a nail.” One discipline's models give you one hammer. His answer was to learn the main ideas of several fields well enough to use them.

What the research says Moderate

Comparing cases across domains improves transfer of principles (48% vs 19%, Gentner, Loewenstein and Thompson, 2003), and imagining failure first surfaces more risks (Klein, 2007). Moderate. This is also why the Field Series maps whole fields, not just tricks.

Drop this myth

“Invert, always invert” is often credited to Munger as his own. He was quoting the 19th-century mathematician Carl Jacobi, and said so.

Run it · 5 min

Take one real decision this week (a price, a hire, a market). Look at it through three models from different fields: incentives, base rates, compounding. Then invert: write three ways to guarantee it fails, and check you're not doing any of them.

“Before I decide, I ask: what would guarantee this fails? Then I make sure I'm not doing it.”

Morning: aim the day

Two drills that decide the day before it decides you. The if-then plan is the best-supported tool in this whole guide.

If-then + StackStrong
Morning · 3 min · behaviour-change family: 1 Goals and planning; 7 Associations; 8 Repetition and substitution
  1. Name an existing reliable anchor: after coffee, opening the laptop, or brushing teeth.
  2. Write: ‘If I finish [anchor] in [place], then I immediately do [one action] for [duration].’ Rehearse once.
  3. Add a coping plan: ‘If the normal cue is missing, then I use [backup cue].’ Repeat in the same context.

The usual mistake: Writing an if-then plan with a vague cue (‘when I have time’) or an oversized response.

Evidence: d=0.65 in the classic meta-analysis; later effects .27-.66. Gollwitzer & Sheeran (2006); Sheeran, Listrom & Gollwitzer (2024); Lally et al. (2010)

Mindful ResetModerate
Morning · 5 min · behaviour-change family: 11 Regulation; 8 Repetition and substitution
  1. Sit safely; choose breath or body sensation as the anchor.
  2. When attention wanders, label ‘thinking/hearing/feeling’ and return without scolding.
  3. End by naming one cue for applying the same noticing skill during the next task.

The usual mistake: Trying to empty the mind or treating distraction as failure.

Evidence: ES=.38 anxiety at 8 weeks; .22 at 3 to 6 months; .30/.23 depression. Goyal et al. (2014), JAMA Internal Medicine

Stop 2.3 4 min

When emotions hit

A rejected pitch, a rude client, a caller who quits. These three change how the moment lands, without pretending it's fine.

Reappraise the TriggerStrong
During work · 3 min · behaviour-change family: 11 Regulation; 4 Shaping knowledge
  1. Name the event, emotion (0 to 100), and the first interpretation.
  2. Generate a specific alternative meaning that is plausible, not falsely positive; include what the situation makes possible or what information is missing.
  3. Re-rate the emotion and select one action consistent with the revised appraisal.

The usual mistake: Replacing a painful thought with an implausibly positive slogan, which is suppression rather than reappraisal.

Evidence: d=.36 cognitive change; d=.45 perspective taking. Webb, Miles & Sheeran (2012), Psychological Bulletin

The Distanced AdviserModerate
During work · 4 min · behaviour-change family: 13 Identity; 15 Self-belief
  1. Write the problem in one sentence and name the emotion and immediate urge.
  2. Ask in third person: ‘What should Kevin do next, given what he knows and what he does not know?’
  3. List two perspectives, one possible change over time, and one compromise or next test; choose a reversible action.

The usual mistake: Using third person as emotional suppression or pretending certainty, rather than widening perspective and acknowledging limits.

Evidence: Kross: convergent small experimental effects; Grossmann & Kross: self-distancing eliminated the asymmetry (no single pooled d). Kross et al. (2014); Grossmann & Kross (2014)

The Thought RecordModerate
During work · 8 min · behaviour-change family: 4 Shaping knowledge; 11 Regulation
  1. Write situation, automatic thought, emotion/intensity, and the behavior that followed.
  2. List evidence for and against the thought; identify a thinking error only if it clarifies the evidence.
  3. Write a balanced, testable alternative and one behavioral experiment; review the result later.

The usual mistake: Arguing with the thought abstractly instead of testing a concrete prediction or action.

Evidence: CBT meta-analyses commonly find moderate effects, but no defensible single effect size for the standalone record. Hofmann et al. (2012), CBT meta-analysis

For your sales calls: after a hard "no", run the distanced adviser in third person: "What should Kevin do next, given what he knows and doesn't know?" Then reappraise: the "no" is data about fit, timing or the pitch, not a verdict on you.

Stop 2.4 4 min

Thinking straight

Decisions go wrong in predictable ways: we look for agreement, ignore base rates, and don't imagine failure. One drill for each.

Consider the OppositeModerate
During work · 5 min · behaviour-change family: 4 Shaping knowledge; 6 Comparison of behavior
  1. Write your current conclusion and confidence (0 to 100).
  2. Generate the strongest case for the opposite, including one observation that would support it.
  3. Decide whether the evidence changes confidence; preserve disagreement where the data remain ambiguous.

The usual mistake: Treating the opposite as equally likely by default or generating a token straw man.

Evidence: Significant correction in two experiments; no general pooled effect. Lord, Lepper & Preston (1984)

Outside-View ForecastModerate
Weekly · 8 min · behaviour-change family: 2 Feedback and monitoring; 4 Shaping knowledge
  1. State the decision, reference class and measurable outcome; write a probability before searching for confirming detail.
  2. Find an outside-view base rate and list two inside-view reasons your case differs.
  3. Record the decision, probability, evidence and review date; update incrementally when evidence arrives.

The usual mistake: Using an irrelevant average as a base rate or changing the probability after the outcome without recording why.

Evidence: Training studies report roughly 8 to 10% accuracy improvement in some forecasting settings; general transfer is unproven. Mellers et al. (2014); Atanasov et al. (2020); Kahneman (2011)

Plan PremortemModerate
Weekly · 8 min · behaviour-change family: 1 Goals and planning; 2 Feedback and monitoring
  1. Imagine the plan is six weeks ahead and has failed; write three plausible reasons, including one outside your control.
  2. For each reason, name an early warning signal and a low-cost mitigation.
  3. Revise the plan and explicitly record residual uncertainty; do not turn the exercise into catastrophe rehearsal.

The usual mistake: Listing dramatic disasters without specifying signals, probabilities or mitigations.

Evidence: About 30% improvement in identifying reasons in the foundational prospective-hindsight study; no stable pooled outcome effect. Klein (2007); Veinott, Klein & Wiggins (2010)

Stop 2.5 4 min

Motivation by design

Motivation is not a mood you wait for. It is something you engineer with rewards, commitments and timing.

The Temptation LockModerate
During work · 2 min · behaviour-change family: 7 Associations; 10 Reward and threat
  1. Pair a delayed-benefit task with a genuinely enjoyable stimulus (e.g., one podcast reserved for a walk or admin block).
  2. Make the enjoyable item unavailable outside the target behavior; pre-decide the rule.
  3. Review weekly whether the bundle increased repetitions, then change the reward if it became stale.

The usual mistake: Using an indulgence that is available everywhere, so the bundle loses its contingency.

Evidence: 51% initial increase in visits in the original full treatment; 9 to 27% in successful megastudy interventions. Milkman, Minson & Volpp (2014); Milkman et al. (2021)

The Commitment GateModerate
Weekly · 5 min · behaviour-change family: 10 Reward and threat; 14 Scheduled consequences
  1. Choose one behavior with a clear pass/fail criterion and a realistic window.
  2. Add an external commitment: tell a person, book a non-refundable slot, use a deposit contract, or lock the tempting alternative.
  3. Specify the recovery rule before starting (what happens after a miss) and review outcomes, not intentions.

The usual mistake: Choosing a punitive contract that creates avoidance, concealment or financial harm after an inevitable lapse.

Evidence: No single stable pooled effect for all commitment devices; short- and long-term effects are positive but heterogeneous. Lokhorst et al. (2013); Milkman et al. (2014)

Fresh-Start RestartModerate
Weekly · 5 min · behaviour-change family: 1 Goals and planning; 12 Antecedents
  1. At Monday morning or another meaningful landmark, name one unfinished goal without self-criticism.
  2. Write a single approach goal for the next seven days and schedule its first action.
  3. Treat the landmark as a planning cue, not evidence that motivation has magically reset; check execution on Friday.

The usual mistake: Making a grand resolution instead of attaching a small, scheduled action to the landmark.

Evidence: 7% after birthday, 33% week start, 14% month start, 11% year start, 47% semester start for exercise relative to baseline in reported analyses. Dai, Milkman & Riis (2014), Management Science

Stop 2.6 7 min

Wellbeing that holds up

The well-being practices with real but modest effects. Small numbers, honestly reported, and worth it because they compound. As a nomad, the connection appointment matters more than it looks.

Three-Beat SavorModerate
During work · 2 min · behaviour-change family: 5 Natural consequences; 11 Regulation
  1. Pause at a positive moment and name exactly what is happening (sensory detail, achievement or connection).
  2. Stay with it for three slow breaths; notice what made it possible and let the feeling be present.
  3. Optionally share the moment with someone; do not photograph or multitask through it.

The usual mistake: Turning savoring into performance capture or demanding positive emotion when it is not present.

Evidence: Small-to-moderate pooled effects reported across savoring interventions; no single robust estimate for this 2-minute drill. Greater Good in Action savoring practice; Bryant & Veroff (2007) framework

Specific Gratitude / AppreciationModerate
Evening · 3 min · behaviour-change family: 5 Natural consequences; 9 Comparison of outcomes
  1. Name one person, event or resource and the specific action or feature that helped.
  2. Write why it mattered today and, where appropriate, send a concise thank-you.
  3. Alternate gratitude with self-kindness; never use it to deny real problems or obligation.

The usual mistake: Forcing a gratitude list during grief, coercion or injustice instead of acknowledging the negative reality.

Evidence: g≈.19 well-being (large later synthesis); g≈−.29 symptoms in Cregg & Cheavens. Davis et al. (2016); Cregg & Cheavens (2021)

One Deliberate KindnessModerate
During work · 5 min · behaviour-change family: 3 Social support; 5 Natural consequences
  1. Choose one specific, feasible act that helps a person without violating your boundaries.
  2. Do it anonymously or openly as appropriate; notice the recipient’s needs rather than your image.
  3. Record whether the act was useful and whether the cost was sustainable.

The usual mistake: Confusing kindness with people-pleasing, overgiving or an obligation to tolerate exploitation.

Evidence: δ=.28 actor wellbeing. Curry et al. (2018), Journal of Experimental Social Psychology

Green WalkModerate
During work · 10 min · behaviour-change family: 5 Natural consequences; 11 Regulation
  1. Walk or sit in accessible greenery; keep the route safe and phone out of hand.
  2. Notice five sensory details, then let attention move naturally without forcing calm.
  3. Return to work with one concrete next action; compare mood/stress before and after for two weeks.

The usual mistake: Assuming a particular landscape or 20-minute dose is universally necessary or sufficient.

Evidence: Small, heterogeneous effects; no single stable estimate for a 10-minute walk. Nature-exposure systematic reviews; Greater Good in Action awe/nature practices

Connection AppointmentStrong
Weekly · 10 min · behaviour-change family: 3 Social support; 12 Antecedents
  1. Choose one relationship that matters and schedule a real-time contact, preferably voice or face-to-face.
  2. Ask one curious question, listen without fixing, and disclose one honest update.
  3. Make the next contact specific; track reciprocity and boundaries, not contact count alone.

The usual mistake: Counting likes or shallow messages as equivalent to supportive, reciprocal connection.

Evidence: OR=1.50 survival association; OR=1.91 for complex social integration. Holt-Lunstad, Smith & Layton (2010), PLOS Medicine

Best Possible Self, then Next StepModerate
Weekly · 8 min · behaviour-change family: 1 Goals and planning; 15 Self-belief
  1. Imagine and write a concrete future in which you have worked toward valued goals in work, relationships and health.
  2. Underline two behaviors that made that future possible and one obstacle likely to recur.
  3. Convert one behavior into a scheduled if-then action for this week; keep the future conditional on effort, not fantasy.

The usual mistake: Writing an unbounded fantasy without linking it to obstacles and controllable behavior.

Evidence: d=.325 wellbeing; d=.334 optimism; d=.511 positive affect. Carrillo et al. (2019), PLOS ONE

Stop 2.7 3 min

Evening: close the loop

The strongest drill in the guide, and Franklin's and Seneca's practice in modern form.

The Evidence LedgerStrong
Evening · 3 min · behaviour-change family: 2 Feedback and monitoring
  1. Choose one observable target (minutes practiced, outreach sent, pages read, workout completed).
  2. Record today's value, the target, and one obstacle; mark the trend without judging yourself.
  3. Write the next smallest adjustment and the date you will check it.

The usual mistake: Tracking vague intentions or collecting numbers without using them to change the next action.

Evidence: d=0.40 for goal attainment; d=1.98 for monitoring frequency. Harkin et al. (2016), Psychological Bulletin

Pair it with Stop 1.1. Run the evidence ledger, then Seneca's three questions. Five minutes, every night. If you only keep one thing from this guide, keep this.

Stop 2.8 4 min

What failed the test

The popular advice that didn't survive the evidence, and the fourteen quotes to stop forwarding. Unlearning, applied.

Advice that failed or is oversold

"Mere presence of your phone drains your brain"

The 2017 finding failed a preregistered replication, and a 2024 meta-analysis found no effect (d = -0.02). Moving the phone still helps, because it stops interruptions.

"Work in 25-minute Pomodoros"

A fine timer habit. There is no evidence that 25 and 5 minutes is a special interval.

"Cold showers build toughness and immunity"

In a trial of 3,018 people, cold showers reduced sick-day absence but not the number of days people were ill. No evidence for focus or toughness.

"Join the 5 a.m. club"

No evidence that waking early itself causes success. A consistent time and enough sleep matter more than the hour.

"21 days to a habit"

Median about 66 days, range 18 to 254 (Lally et al., 2010).

"Willpower is a fuel tank"

Ego depletion failed a 23-lab replication (d = 0.04, Hagger et al., 2016).

"Write about your feelings and you'll heal"

Expressive writing has a very small average effect (r about 0.08 across 146 trials) and is not a trauma cure.

"Gratitude fixes depression"

Gratitude has small real benefits; it is not a replacement for treatment.

"Digital detox resets your brain"

Mixed evidence. What you do instead of the phone matters more than the detox.

"Stoic exercises are proven"

Useful prompts with sparse direct trials. They are graded here through reappraisal and self-distancing research, not on their own.

"Visualise success and it comes"

Positive fantasy alone can lower effort; adding the obstacle and a plan (WOOP) is what works.

"Grit and mindset are the secret"

Both real, both modest, both oversold (Credé et al., 2017; Macnamara and Burgoyne, 2023).
