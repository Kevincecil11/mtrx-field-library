Why do a safari website and a Kubrick frame need the same eye?

You build websites for wildlife and safari brands, and you want to direct films. Those look like two careers. Underneath, they use one skill: deciding what a person should see first, what they should feel, and what they should do next. That skill is design, and this course trains the eye before it teaches any style.

Your brief, in your words. Fonts and pairing first. Colour, with Sanzo Wada's combinations. Composition, with the truth about the golden ratio and the rule of thirds. Minimal versus bold. Brand identity. Apple, Jobs and Ive. Caravaggio and Picasso, Kubrick and Spielberg, great architects. And practical use, with real makers, real images and videos. All of it is on the map below.

The research added one thing you did not ask for, and it comes first. Thirteen research passes, including two independent gap audits, found that the outline taught what good design looks like and almost nothing about how designers get there: the brief, how attention really works, value before colour, testing and critique. So Part 1 is the loop before the looks.

Stop 0.2 · Start 8 min

What is design, if it is not decoration?

In 1972 the Eames Office answered a list of questions about design on film. The first answer is still the best working definition, and it is the test every stop in this course uses.

Philosophy · the definition this course uses
"One could describe Design as a plan for arranging elements to accomplish a particular purpose."

Charles Eames, Design Q and A (1972) · Vitra transcript

Three words do the work. Plan: design is decided before it is made. Arranging: the designer rarely invents the elements, they order them. Purpose: without one there is nothing to judge the work against, and that is when decoration takes over. In the same film Eames answers "What are the boundaries of Design?" with "What are the boundaries of problems?"

Argue with it. Is a Caravaggio design by this definition? What was its purpose, and who set it?

Part 1 · Stop 1.1 · Day 1 20 min

What problem are we actually designing?

The questionA client says "make it look premium". What is the real problem, and how do you find it before you open Figma?

Most bad design is not ugly. It is beautiful work on the wrong problem. The fastest way to waste a month, a budget or a short film is to start making before you know who it is for, what should change for them, and how you will know it worked. Designers call the answer a brief, and the best ones treat writing it as the first design decision.

Case file 1.1
Case file
The 1972 New York subway map
Unimark International: Massimo Vignelli and Bob Noorda, with Joan Charysyn
Debut
August 1972: a diagram of straight lines at 45 and 90 degrees, stations as dots
The bet
Clarity over geography: most streets and landmarks removed
The complaints
Stations in the wrong places, beige water, an almost square Central Park
The end
A map committee from 1975; a more geographic map, made with Michael Hertz Associates, in 1979
Partly: a brilliant answer to half the question
Cold open · predict first · 2 min

Designers still call Vignelli's 1972 subway map one of the most beautiful maps ever made. How long do you think New York kept it, and why did it go?

Write your answer and how sure you are, 1 to 5, before you open it.

Reveal the answer

About seven years. Complaints arrived almost at once: riders said stations seemed to be in the wrong places, the water was beige instead of blue, and Central Park looked almost square. The map answered one question brilliantly, which train goes where, and ignored another riders cared about just as much: where am I on the street when I come up the stairs? In 1979 the transit authority replaced it with a more geographic map. Nothing was wrong with the craft. The brief was incomplete.

The deep answer

Start with what a brief is not. It is not a list of deliverables (homepage, logo, three banners) and it is not a mood ("premium", "clean", "cinematic"). A working brief answers six questions before anyone designs: who is trying to do what, and what stops them now; what should they feel, understand or do afterwards; what is fixed (budget, deadline, technology, brand rules); what evidence would count as success; who decides; and what could go wrong. Each answer removes a thousand possible designs, which is exactly the point.

Then reframe the request as a question you can design for. Designers often write it as "How might we...?" "Redesign this safari site" becomes "How might a first-time safari traveller compare lodges, seasons and conservation impact without losing the feeling of wonder?" Notice what the frame does. It names a person, a task and an emotion, and it already rules out the gallery-of-pretty-photos answer. The frame is where most of the thinking happens; the visuals come out of it.

Charles Eames put the attitude into one line in the 1972 film Design Q and A: there are always design constraints, and they often imply an ethic. In his longer answer he lists the constraints a designer must recognise, "price, size, strength, balance, surface, time", and says the designer works within them with enthusiasm. Constraints are not the enemy of the idea. They are the shape of the problem. A lodge with weak rural internet, guests reading on phones in bright sun and a booking engine you cannot replace is not a limitation on your design. It is your design brief.

The subway map shows what happens when one question crowds out another. Vignelli and Noorda asked: how do we make a tangled system legible? Their answer, a clean diagram with colour-coded routes at fixed angles, is still taught. But the transit authority's riders were also asking: how does the underground connect to the street? Michael Hertz, whose firm drew the 1979 replacement, later said riders criticised the old map for not helping them relate the subway to the ground above, and gave an example: at 50th Street and Eighth Avenue you must walk a long block east to Broadway, while the diagram drew it as an intersection.

Michael Bierut, for decades a partner at the design firm Pentagram, describes his method as finding the idea in the material: collect everything about the client, find the governing idea, build the system, show it at the size it will live, and revise. The lesson is not to copy his style. It is to make your reasoning visible so a client reacts to a decision, not to your taste. A client can argue with "orientation first, because guests compare three lodges". They can only shrug at "I felt it looked premium".

The words insiders use

Brief

A short document that fixes the audience, the change wanted, the constraints, the proof of success and the decider.

Problem frame

The request rewritten as a design question, often "How might we...?", naming a person, a task and a feeling.

Success criteria

The evidence that will count as winning, agreed before design starts: bookings, time to find a price, a reaction.

Constraint

A limit you must design within. Sort them into fixed, negotiable and unknown before you sketch.

Philosophy
"There are always Design constraints, and these often imply an ethic."

Charles Eames, Design Q and A (1972) · Vitra transcript

A constraint is a promise to someone: a budget is the client's money, a slow connection is a guest's patience, a contrast rule is a reader's eyesight. Treating constraints as ethics stops you from designing for your portfolio instead of for them.

Argue with it. Which constraint on your last MTRX™ project did you quietly ignore because it spoiled the look? Who paid for that?

Director's eye · for No. 08 and No. 09

A scene has a brief too, and directors who skip it get pretty shots that do nothing. "Make it moody" is a request. "How might the visual world make her isolation visible before the script names it?" is a frame, and it tells the production designer, the cinematographer and the actor the same thing.

Watch how a brief turns into decisions: a wide lens to make the room too big for one person, one chair at the table, the window light falling everywhere except on her. Each choice is checkable against the frame. That is the difference between style and direction.

Frame drill. Pick one scene you want to shoot. Write its frame as one "How might..." sentence, then list three visual decisions it forces and one it forbids.

Studio lens · MTRX™. Run every MTRX™ discovery call off the six cards: who, change, fixed, proof, decider, risk. Then send the client a one-page brief with your frame at the top and ask them to sign it before design starts. Your safari and wildlife leads will read this as seniority, not paperwork, and it gives you the answer when round three of revisions starts: does this change serve the frame?

Try it · 5 min

Take a real request from your pipeline (a lodge, a photographer, a dental clinic). Write three frames for it: a business frame, a user frame and an emotional frame. Underline the one assumption each would need to test.

Part 1 · Stop 1.2 · Day 2 20 min

How do designers get from fog to a decision?

The questionEvery project starts as fog: a hundred possible directions and a client who wants to see something on Friday. How do professionals get from that to one design they can defend?

The amateur's process is: have an idea, polish it, defend it. The professional's process is wider at the start and stricter at the end. They look before they decide, generate more options than feel comfortable, and kill most of them on evidence. That loop, not talent, is why their work survives contact with clients and audiences. And the founding document of India's best design school is a perfect example of it, written about the city you live in.

Case file 1.2
Case file
The India Report, April 1958
Charles and Ray Eames, at the invitation of the Government of India, sponsored by the Ford Foundation
The ask
Recommend design training to aid small industries and resist the decline in the quality of consumer goods
The method
Three months touring India: centres of design, handicrafts and manufacture, and many conversations
The example
The lota, analysed factor by factor before any form is drawn
The result
The National Institute of Design, Ahmedabad, established in 1961
Holds: research before form
Cold open · predict first · 2 min

Asked to recommend how India should train designers, the Eameses picked one everyday object as their example of good design. Guess the object, and what they said you must do before designing it.

Write your answer and how sure you are, 1 to 5, before you open it.

Reveal the answer

The lota, the simple brass or copper water vessel. They called it perhaps the greatest and most beautiful of all the objects they saw in India. Then they asked how one would go about designing a lota, and answered: first shut out all preconceived ideas, then consider factor after factor. The amount of liquid to carry, the hands that hold it, whether it travels on the head, hip or in a basket, its balance full and empty, how it pours, fills and cleans. Research before form.

The deep answer

In 2004 Britain's Design Council began sharing a picture of how design teams actually work: two diamonds side by side. The first diamond is about the problem. You go wide (Discover: watch people, interview, collect), then narrow (Define: cluster what you found and write the brief). The second diamond is about the solution. You go wide again (Develop: many routes, not one), then narrow (Deliver: prototype, test, reject, ship). The Council is careful to call it a map, not a recipe, and its 2019 framework adds principles such as putting people first and the blunt instruction to iterate, iterate, iterate.

The diamond shape is the point. Going wide feels unproductive, which is why beginners skip it. But the width is where you find what nobody has told you: that guests book safaris on their phones at night, that the lodge manager's real worry is cancellations, that the guide knows a story about a leopard that no competitor tells. Then the narrowing is ruthless. A good Define phase ends with one written problem, and a good Develop phase ends with most ideas dead, each killed by a reason you can say out loud.

The Eameses did exactly this in India, decades before anyone drew diamonds. The Government of India asked them for a programme of design training. They spent three months travelling, looking at centres of design, crafts and manufacture, and talking to people in industry, architecture and education. Only then did they write. Their report, dated April 1958, argued for designers who solve problems rooted in real experience, and the lota passage is its demonstration: a list of factors, from the hands that hold it to the fluid dynamics of pouring while walking. The National Institute of Design opened in Ahmedabad in 1961.

There is a fair attack on all this, and you should hear it. In a 2017 talk the Pentagram designer Natasha Jen called design thinking bullsh*t. Her target was the version sold in workshops: a neat five-step diagram, walls of sticky notes, and the promise that a short course produces design judgement. She later clarified that the problem is the marketing, not thinking like a designer. Take her point seriously: a process diagram never replaces craft, domain knowledge or responsibility. Never run an empathy workshop with no decision attached, and never call a brainstorm research.

Research also changes what you can see. John Snow's map of the 1854 cholera deaths around Broad Street in London is a design that was a research instrument. By marking each death as a bar beside its house, Snow made a cluster visible around one public water pump, and the map helped turn the question from "what is in the air?" to "which water connects these cases?" Look at the plate: the design is almost nothing, just a street plan and black marks. The insight is in what was chosen to be shown.

The words insiders use

Divergence

The going-wide half of a diamond: generating observations or options without judging them yet.

Convergence

The narrowing half: clustering, choosing and killing options, each for a reason you can state.

Design research

Observation, interviews, audits and evidence logs. Not a moodboard of attractive references.

Iteration

Making, testing and revising in loops. Each version exists to teach you something about the next.

Philosophy
"Of all the objects we have seen and admired during our visit to India, the Lota, that simple vessel of everyday use, stands out as perhaps the greatest, the most beautiful."

Charles and Ray Eames, The India Report (1958) · NID scan of the report

Nobody signed the lota. Generations of users refined it against real constraints until it fit the hand, the hip and the well. The Eameses held it up as the standard a trained designer should reach on purpose, through research, in a fraction of the time.

Argue with it. Is a design that evolved with no designer better or worse evidence of good design than one a studio drew in a month?

Director's eye · for No. 08 and No. 09

Film has its own Discover phase and the best directors protect it: location scouting, period research, objects, behaviour, sound. The look of a film is found, not invented. If your references are all other films, your film will look like a copy of a copy.

Develop wide before you commit. Try three visual grammars for the same scene, for example tight frames, wide empty frames and unsteady handheld, and shoot or sketch each one. Pixar's Braintrust, as Ed Catmull describes it, gives candid notes after screenings but has no power to order the director: notes widen, the director narrows.

Frame drill. Take one scene and sketch it three ways: compressed, expansive and unstable. Write one line on what each grammar makes the audience feel, then pick one and say what killed the other two.

Studio lens · MTRX™. Put a real Discover phase into every MTRX™ proposal, even a small one: a 30-minute call with the lodge manager, one with a past guest, a look at three competitors and a note of what the guide would say. Then show the client three routes labelled by behaviour, not mood: orientation first, immersion first, proof first. You sell the thinking, and the revision rounds get shorter.

Try it · 5 min

Set a five-minute timer and draw 20 rough thumbnails for one hero section: five different stories, four layouts each. Circle three, and next to each write the evidence or constraint that earned the circle.

Go wide before you decide, then kill ideas for reasons you can say out loud.
